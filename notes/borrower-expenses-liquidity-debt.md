# Borrower expenses, liquidity, and debt

Expenses of a borrower are not fixed. An increase (decrease) in liquidity, such as when a bank increases (decreases) a credit-card limit, leads to an increase (decrease) in consumer spending. [[1]](#1)

Income expectations also matter. Following an increase (decrease) in income, people tend to raise (lower) their expectations of future income, leading to higher (lower) spending. These expectations tend to overshoot: people extrapolate temporary income changes into the future, causing spending to exceed what their eventual long-run income would justify. [[2]](#2)

The Danish high-cost consumption-loan study finds that borrowers already had expenditures substantially above income 12 months before taking their first high-cost loan, with the gap widening as borrowing approached. Importantly, the pattern does not appear to be driven primarily by sudden shocks such as unexpected expenses or income losses, suggesting persistent spending above current income rather than a temporary financial setback. [[3]](#3)

The absence of a sudden shock raises the question of why some borrowers persistently spend above income. Spending above current income is not necessarily problematic: someone may rationally draw down savings to finance valued present consumption. The concern is persistent debt-financed overspending, which may reflect poor financial literacy or planning, overoptimistic income expectations, and personality traits. Research links lower conscientiousness and higher extraversion, as well as traits associated with present bias, temptation spending, and gambling, to riskier borrowing and financial behavior. [[3]](#3)[[4]](#4)

Personality also affects how borrowers respond to financial distress. More assertive/extraverted borrowers are more likely to approach lenders and negotiate refinancing or restructuring rather than default, while less assertive borrowers are more likely to fall into delinquency or default. [[4]](#4)

Even without observing personality, lenders can learn from how the borrower arrived at the current debt level: was the gap financed by savings, a temporary shock, or repeated new borrowing?

There are two cases. If income exceeds expenses before debt payments, the borrower can theoretically repay all lenders over time, but limited cash flow creates a musical-chairs game. If expenses already exceed income before debt payments, the borrower is structurally cash-flow negative, so lenders collectively lose unless income rises or expenses fall. In this case, refinancing may simply postpone or transfer the loss.

It also matters which loan gets paid. Borrowers do not necessarily default proportionally across creditors; they prioritize some debts over others, creating competition among lenders. [[5]](#5) Features that make a lending relationship valuable—such as preserving access to additional credit—may therefore affect which creditor gets paid. For example, a borrower might continue paying a lender that provides a valuable ongoing credit line while defaulting on another lender, even if the latter has a larger outstanding balance. This creates a potential “musical chairs” dynamic in which lenders compete for repayment.
