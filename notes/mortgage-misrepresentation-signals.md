# Mortgage misrepresentation as a credit-risk signal

Two mortgage studies show that small acts of misrepresentation can be strong signals of future credit problems. Garmaise finds that borrowers reported assets just above round-number thresholds that affected loan eligibility unusually often, suggesting that some deliberately overstated their assets to qualify. These borrowers were almost 25 percentage points more likely to become delinquent (against a roughly 20% baseline), with the increase exceeding 40 percentage points when assets were unverified. [[1]](#1)

A different form of misrepresentation is studied by Elul, Payne, and Tilson: borrowers who were actually real-estate investors sometimes claimed to be owner-occupants in order to obtain cheaper mortgage rates. This was not a rare fringe behavior: fraudulent borrowers represented about one-third of the effective investor population. They defaulted at a 75% higher rate than comparable declared investors, and their defaults appeared more likely to be strategic. Thus, these borrowers were not simply financially incompetent: they deliberately manipulated information to obtain better terms and, when circumstances worsened, appeared more willing to strategically default. [[2]](#2)
